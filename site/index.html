CHAPTER 1

A good prediction is a probability, not a pick

Saying “the Chiefs will win” is easy to say and almost impossible to judge. A better forecaster says “the Chiefs win 64% of games like this one.” Then you can check, over hundreds of games, whether 64% really means about 64 wins out of 100.

To score probabilities I use log loss. It rewards being right, and it punishes being confidently wrong far more than being cautiously wrong. Lower is better. Always saying “50/50” scores 0.693, so any useful model has to beat that.

In plain EnglishIf you said 90% and the home team lost, you took a big hit. If you said 60% and they lost, you took a small one. That's what makes honest, calibrated probabilities the best strategy.
CHAPTER 2

The ingredients: what the model is allowed to know

📈 Play-by-play and EPA

Every NFL play since 1999, each scored with EPA (expected points added): how many points that play added to or took from the drive. Average EPA per play is a much better read on a team than the final score, which is full of luck.

🧑‍🎓 Quarterbacks

The quarterback who takes the first snap is the starter, and each QB carries his own rating from team to team. A backup starting is a big deal, and the model sees it.

🩹 Injuries and inactives

Weekly injury reports and game-day inactive lists, turned into “how much of the roster's usual snaps are missing.” I tested these carefully; spoiler in Chapter 11.

🎲 Betting lines: opponent only

The betting market is never an input. It is the benchmark, the best forecaster I can compare against. Models that use the market are a different project.

The one ruleA prediction for a game may only use information from before that game was played. It sounds obvious. Chapter 5 shows how easily it gets broken.
CHAPTER 3

Rating teams that keep changing

How good is a team? You never see it directly. You only see noisy results: one game they dominate, the next they escape with a fluke win. A Kalman filter is a recipe for keeping a running belief: after every game, move your estimate toward what you just saw, by an amount that depends on how sure you were.

  • If you're unsure about a team (early season), a game moves you a lot.
  • If you're confident (late season), one game barely moves you.
  • Between seasons, rosters change, so everyone is pulled back toward average and you become less sure.

Below is a simplified, one-number version of the idea, running on real 2025 games. Drag the sliders and watch how “trusting each game more or less” changes the picture.

In plain EnglishDots are what happened in each game. The green line is my running belief about the team. If you tell it that single games are very noisy, it barely budges. If you tell it single games are gospel, it chases every result.
CHAPTER 4

Quarterbacks change everything (sort of)

The full model rates three things per team: offense (without the QB), defense, and each quarterback separately. When a team swaps quarterbacks, the model swaps the rating too. Did it help? A little, but the evidence isn't as strong as it first looked.

What I foundThe QB layer improves log loss slightly (its interval just touches zero) and most clearly in games where a team's starter changed. But once you account for the many ideas I tested (Chapter 11), it's suggestive rather than proven. In the 111 playoff games it pointed the wrong way, which is too few games to say anything either way.
CHAPTER 5

The easiest way to fool yourself: peeking at the future

My first version of this project scored suspiciously well, as good as Las Vegas. The reason was leakage: it rated every team using full-season statistics and then “predicted” games from earlier in that very season. It had read the answer key.

The demo below recreates the mistake with nothing but public EPA data. Flip the switch.

How I stop it from happening again

Every feature in the pipeline has a perturbation test: I scramble every result that happened after a game, rebuild the features, and check that the prediction for that game didn't change by even a decimal. If a number changes, something is peeking, and the test fails.

I also split time into a development window (where I select ideas) and a holdout window (to score them). I was not that disciplined from day one, as the next chapter admits.

CHAPTER 6

Testing like it's the real thing: walk-forward

To predict season S, the model only gets to learn from seasons before S, exactly like real life. I repeat that for every season, then add up the scores. This is called walk-forward validation. Slide through the seasons:

🧪 Development window

Seasons 2002–2016, where ideas are selected (since Oct 6). Settings like “how fast do ratings forget?” were tuned on 2011–2016 only, then frozen.

🔒 Holdout window

Seasons 2017–2025: 2,494 games with a betting line, including 111 playoff games. Every idea was scored here, so it is not sealed: the first model and about half the experiments were built when this was the only test window (Sep 30), and the formal split came on Oct 6. That is why I correct for how many looks I took, and why I replicated the main result on 2003–2009 (Chapter 11).

CHAPTER 8

Simulating the playoffs

Single-game odds are only half the job. To get a team's chance of winning the Super Bowl you play out the bracket, with seeding, byes, home field and reseeding, thousands of times. Even a team that's favored in every game has a modest chance of winning it all, because it has to win three or four in a row.

This is the actual 2025 field with my model's game probabilities from before the playoffs began. Run it once to watch, or thousands of times for the odds, and then compare with what really happened.

What's “uncertainty in team strength”?My ratings are estimates, not truths. In each simulated season, every team gets a random nudge up or down, sized by how unsure the filter was about it. That makes a team's fortunes linked across rounds (a team that's secretly great tends to win several in a row), which is how real brackets behave.
CHAPTER 9

A time machine: the January 2026 playoffs

Chapter 8 simulated a bracket. Here is the honest test: I rewound the model to every week of the 2025 season (whose playoffs were played in January 2026) and asked, each time, what bracket would you draw if you had to draw it today? Every projection uses only games before that week. Then I scored the projections against what actually happened.

The bar to clear is a boring one: just look at the standings. If my simulation can't beat “whoever is in a playoff spot right now,” it isn't earning its keep.

CHAPTER 10

Could it have made money?

A betting price is a probability in disguise, plus the bookmaker's cut. Try it:

I then ran the most honest test I could: bet whichever side my model thinks is underpriced, using the closing line (the sharpest one), across the whole holdout. I chose the settings on the development years first and looked at the holdout once.

What I foundEvery version lost money, about 9% of each stake on average, and staking by the Kelly rule wiped out the bankroll. Betting every favorite also loses (the bookmaker's cut), and the model's picks aren't statistically different from that. A formal check, the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, comes out at 0.00: no evidence of skill after accounting for how many strategies I tried.

CHAPTER 11

Thirty-seven ideas, one survivor

I tried lots of ideas: rest days, bye weeks, injuries, game-day inactives, garbage-time filtering, margin models, recency weighting… Each one gets a score: how much did it change log loss, and how sure am I? Negative means better.

But here's the trap: test enough ideas and some will look great by pure luck. Try the simulator first.

That's why every experiment is logged in a registry, and the whole family is corrected for multiple testing (Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg). Here's the full list:

What I foundFour ideas looked significant on their own, where luck alone would produce about two. Only one held up after correction: the Kalman rating filter, with its memory across seasons, which beat simple season-to-date EPA. I then replicated it on seasons that had no part in its design (below): same direction, smaller, and short of significance on its own. Everything else (rest, byes, neutral sites, injuries, pass/rush splits) failed to show a detectable effect. Negative results are results.
CHAPTER 12

Why “no effect” is often “can't tell”

Game results are noisy, so detecting a small improvement takes a lot of games. This calculator shows the chance a test would catch a real improvement of a given size.

In plain EnglishWith 111 playoff games, only a huge improvement (about 0.027 log loss) would reliably show up. A realistic improvement of 0.002 would need around 70 seasons of games to confirm. That's why I say I failed to detect an effect rather than “proved there isn't one.”
CHAPTER 13

The forward test: predictions I can't edit

Backtests can be accidentally flattered. The only fully honest test is to write down predictions before the games and score them afterward. Every week during the 2026 season the model logs its picks to a public ledger. Each row contains a fingerprint of the previous row (a hash chain), so quietly changing history is impossible without breaking every later row.

CHAPTER 14

What I learned

Limits and honest caveats

  • The holdout (2,494 games, only 111 in the playoffs) can detect only fairly large improvements; small real effects may hide inside the noise.
  • I compare to closing lines, the market's final and most informed price. Earlier lines may be less efficient; I did not test that.
  • The holdout was examined for every idea I tried, so it is not sealed. Hyperparameters were tuned on 2011–2016 only, results are corrected for multiple looks, and a few diagnostics (for example the recency-weighting test) are labeled “post hoc.”
  • The forward test has only a handful of games so far. Early numbers are noise.
